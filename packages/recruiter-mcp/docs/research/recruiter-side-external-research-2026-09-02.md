# The recruiter's day and the assistant: external research

Research date: 2026-09-02. Method: four breadth-first research subagents with a fixed budget of 15 web calls each, plus two adversarial verification passes on the claims that would change the recommendation. Every subagent spent its budget; none was truncated silently. Claims that could not be corroborated from a second source are marked as unverified in the text rather than softened into prose. A companion document, the recruiter-side capability map, covers our own server's recipes, withheld tools, and write surface.

## 1. Where a recruiter's week goes, and what the vendors sell instead

The only primary time study that could be opened tracked more than 200 recruiters across 20 companies for two weeks. Interview coordination took 28 percent of the week, data entry into the tracking system 14 percent, status and follow-up email 10 percent, hiring-manager coordination 12 percent, meetings 8 percent, and the work recruiters are paid to do, sourcing, screening and interviewing, 28 percent. [1] The study was run by a recruiting vendor, so the split is directional rather than precise, but its shape is corroborated by the re-quoted figures everywhere else: scheduling is the single largest block, hygiene and status-chasing the second, and recruiting itself under a third. Recruiters who use generative assistants report saving about a fifth of the week and putting the time back into screening and skills assessment. [2]

No study measures debrief preparation, offer work, or reporting as their own line. That matters because those are exactly the tasks the vendors now advertise. Greenhouse's published prompts for recruiters are a pipeline-velocity report, a pre-debrief candidate review, and a compensation-mismatch check against posted bands. [3] Ashby's are candidate comparison before a debrief, interviewer calibration, pipeline health, and compensation expectations. [4] The high-frequency questions a recruiter or coordinator has on a Tuesday, who owes a scorecard, which interviews this week have an interviewer who declined, what is stuck past two weeks, which requisitions have no posting, appear only in the coordinator and operations sections of those same pages. An assistant for this team should be indexed on the coordinator's list, and those questions are counts and aging, which should be computed deterministically with the model narrating the result.

Recruiters draw the trust line consistently across every survey found: logistics yes, judgment no. Greenhouse's own research puts hiring-manager trust in AI decisions at 70 percent and candidate perception of fairness at 8 percent; Gartner reports 26 percent of candidates trust AI to evaluate them fairly; Dice reports net trust of minus 53 percent for AI-only hiring against plus 70 percent for human-driven processes. [5][6][7] No survey measures whether a recruiter trusts the assistant's answer about their own pipeline. The nearest proxy is Ashby's caveat on its own assistant: language models produce confident but incorrect analysis when they need to count, aggregate, or compare large datasets, and reporting where accuracy matters should use deterministic reports. [4]

## 2. What the assistants on the market actually do

Greenhouse's official connector supports both reading and writing, under each user's own permissions, with scopes set per data category by a Site Admin. Two guardrails cannot be overridden: every delete is permanently blocked, and five actions require explicit human confirmation, rejecting an applicant, hiring one, merging candidates, anonymizing a candidate, and bulk-revoking user permissions. [8][9] Every other write a scope enables runs with whatever confirmation the AI client imposes, and no undo is documented. Of Greenhouse's sixteen published example prompts across five roles, thirteen read, one writes to Slack, and two write to Greenhouse; both Greenhouse writes are job-level hygiene, a setup-checklist fix and a priority custom-field update, and both belong to coordinators and leaders rather than recruiters. [3] Greenhouse publishes no tool list; the widely repeated count of 36 tools comes from a competitor's write-up, and its claim that the connector schedules interviews is unverified. [10]

Ashby's connector is the cleanest published boundary. Its documentation lists sixteen tools as of this date, twelve reads and four writes: create a candidate, add a note (drafted for confirmation before saving), change an application's stage, and consider a candidate for a job. Nothing among the documented tools sends email, schedules an interview, rejects a candidate, or writes an offer. [11] The figure of 38 tools that circulates for Ashby belongs to community-built servers, not Ashby's own. Ashby publishes two rate limits, 120 requests per minute per token and 120 tool budget units per minute per user and organization. [11][12]

Gem's agents draft outreach for the recruiter to send, score and rank applicants without deciding, and flag suspected fraud without rejecting. [13] The autonomous writers in the market are candidate-facing scheduling bots and sourcing outreach, which is where the published incidents happened. Across every product, the universal write set is small and internal-facing: add a note, move a stage, create a candidate or application, update a field. Candidate-facing side effects are the boundary line the vendors drew, and no product documents an undo.

The market's blind spot on attribution is Greenhouse's own: its audit log tags connector calls with an event type, but the change log in the app shows AI-made writes under the person's name with no indicator, and the audit row names the OAuth client by identifier rather than by product. [9] An assistant that writes should stamp its own provenance on the record.

## 3. Our six analyses against the market's definitions

Every vendor measures stage dwell as calendar days from stage entry to stage exit, per stage and per job, and leaves the "too long" threshold to the customer. Greenhouse's time-in-stage report scores the share of stage transitions completed within a customer-set goal, excludes the Application Review and Hiring Manager Review stages, and defaults to a 180-day window. [14] Gem's status indicators count hours since a status was applied, with a warning tier and a missed tier, and Gem states outright that email or other activity does not reset the clock. [15] Nobody publishes a seven-day default. The two names the market uses for owed feedback are feedback completion and pending feedback, reported as a rate per interviewer; nobody calls it accountability. [16][17] Source analysis is titled source of hire and anchored on the hire date. Rejection-reason analysis is a distribution per reason, job, and stage; no vendor ships drift.

Read against that vocabulary, the six recipes come out as follows. Stage latency: keep, add the median beside the mean, make the seven-day threshold a per-stage-class goal the caller can override, and exclude or separately label Application Review. Scorecard accountability: rename to feedback completion, keep the owed list ranked by owner, and add the rate. Interview feedback drag: rename to feedback turnaround, state the service level in hours with a warning tier and a missed tier, and report the median plus the share over the level. Pipeline quality: keep the status mix and the pass-through rate, and redefine the stale-by-last-activity component, which matches no vendor's definition and which Gem explicitly rejects as a clock. Source quality: keep, title it source of hire, anchor the window on the hire date, and add the application-to-hire rate per source. Rejection reason drift: rename to rejection reasons, keep concentration as the headline, and present drift as an explicit comparison of this window with the prior one. The phrases stale requisition, requisition with no activity, and missing owner appear in none of the vendor documentation; they are our vocabulary, not the market's, which does not make them wrong.

## 4. What a write can set off, and how the market governs it

The decision-changing fact of this research is from Greenhouse's own API reference: a successful stage move through the API fires any configured stage transition rules, including automated emails. [18] Those rules, configured per job by whoever set up the pipeline, can send an availability request, a self-schedule link, a candidate email, a form, an e-signature agreement, an identity-verification request, or a take-home test, and they run whenever a candidate enters the stage, with no carve-out for API moves. [19][20] The preconditions are that the candidate has an email address and is not marked Do Not Email. The API exposes no way to read a stage's transition rules in advance, so a caller cannot know what a move will trigger until it has triggered. By contrast, the interview endpoints record an already-scheduled event rather than creating one and send nothing, [21] the offer endpoints create and update records that a person must still send from the app, [22] and a rejection emails the candidate only when the call includes a rejection-email object with a template. [23]

Our stage-move action is already classed as high-impact and gated on a separate entitlement, its preview says that transition rules including candidate email may run, and it calls the documented endpoint. [24] Two things about it are unverified: it never passes a from-address, so any rule email would come from the organization's default sender rather than the acting recruiter, and it passes an application-stage row identifier where the reference is silent on which identifier type is expected. The one live write ever applied through our plane was a note; a stage move has been previewed but never applied against the real tenant.

The governance pattern every platform vendor converged on has four parts: classify tools as read, write, or destructive when they are registered; run reads silently; render writes as a plain-language preview and stop; and make the confirmation a separate step bound to the exact arguments, with guardrails re-checked immediately before execution. The protocol's own tool annotations default to the cautious reading, so an unannotated tool is assumed non-read-only, destructive, and non-idempotent, and the annotations are advisory, since a server can lie and they do nothing against prompt injection. [25][26] Anthropic's guidance adds the batch hazard: confirmation must happen before each block, because a batch can complete a multistep action in one turn. [27]

The recorded incidents are recipient-set and template errors on bulk or automated sends, not model hallucination: a rejection email that shipped with its generation prompt still inside, a rejection that copied around sixty candidates instead of blind-copying them, a university that sent its admit letter to 29,000 rejected applicants. [28][29][30] The two AI-specific incidents are prompt injection through a candidate's public profile, where text in a LinkedIn bio was obeyed by an outreach bot and sent back to the candidate, once in 2025 and again in May 2026, and resume-borne injection against screening models is now a measured research subject. [31][32][33] No published incident of an assistant moving or rejecting the wrong candidate was found; absence of a write-up is not absence of the risk, since the incidents that get written up are the ones a candidate makes public.

None of the three tracking-system vendors auto-closes a requisition. All three treat a reminder to a human as the automation and leave the state change to a person: Greenhouse sends scorecard reminders automatically and lets admins send them by hand, Gem emails interviewers an hour after the scheduled interview, Ashby ships an alerts library and automated candidate scheduling reminders. [34][35][36]

## 5. Implications for the recruiter side of the Greenhouse MCP

The evidence points at a different investment than the People Ops side. There, the value was in new aggregate analysis. Here, recruiters gain more from four things that are not analysis at all.

The first is the coordinator's question list, computed deterministically. Who owes me a scorecard, which interviews this week have a declined or silent interviewer, which of my candidates have sat in a stage past its goal, which of my requisitions have no posting or no interview kit. Most of these are one read plus a count, and the evidence says they are the daily questions; three of them are already partly served by existing recipes under other names.

The second is the withheld read tools, none of which cites a constraint: approvals, interview kits and job interviews, prospect pools, tracking links, tags, and the rest. A recruiter who can edit a job note but cannot list one, or who cannot see the approval chain holding an offer, has a gap the code created rather than the API.

The third is the write plane's honesty. Our stage move is gated correctly, but the preview cannot name what the destination stage will fire, because the API does not expose it. The defensible design is the one the market uses: treat a move into any stage as a candidate-facing action, preview it by name, require a separate confirmation bound to the exact arguments, cap batches and enumerate every candidate in them, default rejections to no email with a delayed send when email is chosen, and stamp our own provenance on the record because Greenhouse will not. Before any stage move is applied in production it should be applied once on a sandbox candidate marked Do Not Email in a stage that carries a rule, and the observed side effects recorded as the ground truth.

The fourth is renaming and redefining the six analyses to the market's words and clocks, so a recruiter who has used Ashby or Gem recognizes what they are looking at, and so a leadership number computed here matches one computed there.

What should not be built is the thing the vendors' marketing pages lead with. Pre-debrief candidate packets and compensation checks are analytically rich and infrequent; the record reads they need already exist on our surface and on Greenhouse's own connector, and the assistant can compose them from those reads without a dedicated recipe. Autonomous scheduling and autonomous outreach are where the published incidents live, and Ashby's documented tool list excludes both on purpose.

## Unverified and open questions

- The recruiter time split rests on one vendor-run study; SHRM, Bersin, McKinsey and Lighthouse time studies were not reached.
- Greenhouse's connector tool list and count (36) are unpublished; scheduling writes through it are a third-party claim.
- Whether an API stage move with no from-address sends rule emails from a global sender, and which identifier type the move endpoint expects for the current stage, are unverified; a sandbox apply settles both.
- No published incident of an assistant moving or rejecting the wrong candidate was found; the risk is inferred from the write surface, not from a case.
- Ashby's advanced report catalog and Lever's dashboard help center did not load; their metric definitions are from snippets or prior knowledge.
- Culture Amp's reporting-group minimum (5) is the only vendor-stated small-cell threshold found; it is stated for surveys, not pipelines.

Fan-out: four breadth-first subagents at 15 calls each, all spent; two skeptics at 10 calls each, both spent. No subagent was truncated.
